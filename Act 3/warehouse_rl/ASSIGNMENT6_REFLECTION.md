# Assignment 6: Advanced Optimizations Reflection

In my initial ten-episode benchmark, the skeleton_optimization agent finished eighth out of eight agents with an average profit of $1,102.78, a 95% confidence interval of ±$1,460.30, and a completion rate of 25.4%. After adding the staffing and package-pickup optimizations, the agent finished first out of eight with an average profit of $27,069.67, a 95% confidence interval of ±$4,005.85, and a completion rate of 58.9%. This was a profit improvement of $25,966.89, an increase of 33.5 percentage points in completion rate, and a rise of seven positions. The initial and final profit intervals did not overlap, which gives strong evidence that the improvement was not just a small random difference between episodes. However, the final interval was still wide, so the agent’s performance was not equally consistent in every episode. This assignment showed me that warehouse optimization involves competing goals. Hiring more employees can increase service speed but also raises labor costs, while hiring a manager helps the layout strategy but adds an even higher wage. The agent therefore had to balance profit, service speed, employee utilization, and operating cost instead of maximizing only one measurement.

I replaced random staffing with an economic strategy that checks the number of waiting orders against the number of available workers. In plain English, the agent hires when demand is high and the warehouse is profitable enough to afford another wage. It fires only when demand is low, financial pressure exists, and enough workers will remain to operate the warehouse. Different hiring and firing thresholds prevent the agent from repeatedly reversing its decisions, and the strategy maintains one manager without hiring unnecessary additional managers. For package pickup, I replaced random assignments with a matching algorithm that compares every idle worker with the pending orders. Each possible match receives a score based mainly on walking distance, while also considering order value, waiting time, priority, and deadline urgency. The Hungarian algorithm selects the group of assignments with the highest combined benefit, with each worker and order used only once. During development, the staffing-only version averaged $24,487.72 across three episodes, while the combined staffing and routing version averaged $25,433.22 across five episodes. Because these tests used different episode samples, I treated the increase as a useful refinement signal rather than proof that routing alone caused the entire difference. The algorithms complement one another because the staffing strategy provides enough workers to control demand while preserving one manager for useful layout changes. A relocation creates a short-term manager cost, but moving popular products closer to the truck bays produces repeated walking-distance savings on future orders. The routing algorithm then uses those shorter distances to make better worker-order matches, while efficient assignments help justify the wages of the workers hired by the staffing algorithm. Together, the three strategies produced stronger results than the layout optimization could achieve by itself.
