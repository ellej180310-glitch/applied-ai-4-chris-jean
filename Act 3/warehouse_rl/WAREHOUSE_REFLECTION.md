# Assignment 5: Warehouse Simulation Reflection
In the initial benchmark, my skeleton_optimization agent finished eighth out of eight agents with an average profit of −$1,009.53 and a 95% confidence interval of ±$4.38. After several testing and improvement cycles, the final agent finished fifth out of eight with an average profit of $6,328.45 and a 95% confidence interval of ±$4,762.70. This was a profit improvement of $7,337.98 and a rise of three positions. The initial and final confidence intervals do not overlap, so the profit increase was large enough to represent a real improvement rather than a small random difference. However, the final confidence interval was wide, which shows that performance still varied considerably between episodes. The completion rate also decreased slightly from 34.2% to 32.0%. This showed me that profit and completion rate are related but do not always improve together. The main objective was to maximize profit, which depends on completing valuable orders while controlling labor and relocation costs. Product placement, employee staffing, and order routing all affect that objective. More workers may increase capacity but also increase salaries, while more layout swaps may shorten future retrieval distances but occupy the manager in the present. My agent became profitable because it handled those cost constraints more carefully while improving the warehouse layout.

I replaced random layout swaps with a two-phase greedy optimization algorithm. In the first phase, the agent uses product-access frequency to identify the top 25% of requested products. It measures each product’s Manhattan distance from the truck bays and searches accessible storage positions for a location that is at least two steps closer. It also avoids replacing a product with one that has an equal or higher access frequency. In the second phase, the agent uses the co-occurrence matrix to find products that are often ordered together. It calculates the benefit of a possible move using co-occurrence frequency multiplied by distance saved and selects the highest-value clustering move. I also made the agent check whether the queue was manageable and whether an idle manager was available before requesting a relocation. During testing, I discovered that the original code incorrectly treated an employee’s target-distance value as proof that a manager existed. This caused the warehouse to hire many unnecessary managers and reach bankruptcy before the layout improvements could provide value. Correcting the manager check allowed the simulation to complete and made the layout strategy financially useful. In the future, I would replace random worker hiring with demand-based staffing, assign orders according to value, urgency, and employee distance, and add cooldown tracking so products are not moved too frequently. These changes could reduce the final confidence interval, improve the completion rate, and make the agent’s profit more consistent.
